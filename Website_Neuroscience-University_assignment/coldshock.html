Background

Cold shock

Cold shock is a reflex that occurs when the body enters cold water, and mostly opposes the diving reflex. After falling into water below ~25°C, peaking at 10 - 15°C, people hyperventilate, gasp for air, have a higher heart rate, hypertension, and peripheral vasoconstriction (Bierens et al., 2016). Peripheral vasoconstriction is the same response as in the diving reflex, but what is interesting is how the diving reflex lowers the heart rate (parasympathetic activation) (Godek & Freeman, 2025), while cold shock increases the heart rate (sympathetic activation) (Bierens et al., 2016).

The mechanisms underlying cold shock and diving reflex differ from each other. Cold shock works on thermoreceptors located on the skin, mostly on the skin of the torso, limbs, and belly. When suddenly exposed to cold water, these thermoreceptors start firing at high rates and directly stimulate the respiratory center and reflexively activate o-motoneurons innervating the intercostal muscles and diaphragm, which makes voluntary breath-holding nearly impossible. This involuntary drive to breathe increases the risk of drowning by causing people to swallow water when submerged. Moreover, when face immersion and maximal breath-holding occur simultaneously, the incidence of cardiac arrhythmias can rise from approximately 2% during head-out immersion to over 80% with full face submersion (Bierens et al., 2016).

Full-face submersion means cold shock, and the divers reflex will be active at the same time. This means that parasympathetic signals are inhibited, the heart rate is lowered, sympathetic signals are exhibited, and the heart rate is stimulated simultaneously. Paton et al. (2005) stated that diving-related arrhythmic events could be enhanced by this signalling because ventricular tachyarrhythmias can be caused by increased cardiac sympathetic activity. Shattock & Tipton (2012) stated that simultaneous signalling of the sympathetic and parasympathetic nervous systems could cause cardiac arrhythmias. This most likely occurs under conditions of cyclical vagal stimulation, concurrent with sympathetic activation. For example, when breathing resumes after an extended period of apnea in cold water. Cyclical vagal stimulation refers to the increase in sympathetic activity during inhalation and parasympathetic activity during exhalation. After a period of apnea, these stimuli are more intense because people inhale more air after a shortage of breath. These stronger stimuli combined with strong sympathetic activation from cold shock produced arrhythmias within 10 s after the cessation of breath-holding in 62 - 82% of healthy participants (Shattock & Tipton, 2012).

Autonomic conflict can cause three different types of cardiac arrhythmias. The most common types are supraventricular (in the atria) and junctional (on the atrioventricular node). These arrhythmias tend to be brief and are usually not dangerous, but they do show how easily the heart rhythm can be destabilized by the rapid switch between sympathetic and vagal dominance (Olshansky et al., 2017). Short periods of ventricular tachycardia, intervened by bradycardia were also observed. These arrhythmias are usually not dangerous but can be a threat to more vulnerable people (Shattock & Tipton, 2012). Although we can speculate, it is difficult to measure the number of fatalities due to these arrhythmias caused by the combination of cold shock and the diving reflex. People sadly do not come with notes of why they died, and dying in the water can be attributed to many different factors, such as hypothermia and asphyxia. If drowning victims are found with water in their lungs, the chances are small; they died from heart arrhythmias because they most probably died from oxygen shortage due to ‘normal drowning.’ When there is still air, the cause of death is often attributed to hypothermia (dying of cold). Heart rhythm disturbances could also be the cause of death but are not detectable post-mortem. These can even cause sudden gasping of air while submerged, which makes it even possible for them to be the cause when water is found inside the lungs of a drowning victim (Shattock & Tipton, 2012).

In conclusion, our evolved instincts do not always work in harmony. When submerged in cold water, the diving reflex slows the heart rate by increasing parasympathetic activity, whereas the cold shock response stimulates the heart rate by increasing sympathetic activity. This conflict can cause potentially dangerous cardiac arrhythmias when breathing resumes after a period of apnea, as the intense cyclical vagal stimulation from deep inhalation and exhalation clashes with the background sympathetic activation. The tragedy in this situation is that the very reflexes we evolved to survive can become the reason we drown under the wrong conditions, and chances are low people will even know what happened post-mortem.

Background

Evolutionary background

The mammalian diving reflex is much stronger in diving mammals than in terrestrial mammals, such as humans. For example, the heart rate of dive-seels drops from 100 bpm to 10 bpm after submerging in water (Williams et al., 2015), while the human heart rate only drops down an average of 25 - 41 bpm from the average heart rate of 85 (Caspers et al., 2011). The fact that this reflex exists in all different types of mammals means that it must have already been present in the early stages of mammal evolution. One hypothesis for why the diving reflex is much stronger in diving mammals is that, by making them able to survive underwater for extended periods of time, diving mammals not only had a lower chance of drowning, but were also able to dive deeper, giving them an advantage by having more food available. If the reflex played a bigger role in the survival of diving mammals than that of terrestrial mammals, it is logical that it developed further in diving animals.

The existence of the diving reflex in both pinnipeds and cetaceans (Williams et al., 2015) highlights another unique aspect of the diving reflex: its development in different diving mammals. Pinnipeds (commonly known as seals) and cetaceans (commonly known as whales, purpoises, and dolphins) evolved from different evolutionary lineages, but both independently developed a highly advanced diving reflex.
